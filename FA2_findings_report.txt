1. SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Goal: predict a song's genre from its Spotify audio features alone (multiclass
classification over 5 genres), then deploy the trained models in a Streamlit app.

Main results:
  * Random Forest is the best model: 86.0% accuracy, 0.854 macro F1 on the
    held-out test set.
  * SVM came second (80.2% accuracy, 0.791 macro F1). Logistic Regression,
    Decision Tree and kNN were all close to 77-78% accuracy.
  * Tuning the Random Forest with GridSearchCV made almost no difference
    (macro F1 0.8532 -> 0.8537). The default settings were already close to
    the best.
  * Classical and heavy-metal are easy to recognise (F1 0.95 and 0.92).
    EDM and hip-hop are the hardest (F1 0.78 and 0.79). They are mostly
    confused with each other and with country.
  * Acousticness is the most useful feature, followed by danceability,
    energy and speechiness.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. DATASET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Source      : Hugging Face, "maharshipandya/spotify-tracks-dataset"
              (collected from the Spotify Web API). A local copy is kept as
              spotify_tracks.csv.
Full size   : 114,000 tracks, 20 columns, 125 genres (1,000 tracks per genre)
Missing     : only 1 row with missing artist/album/track name (text columns
              that are not used by the models)
Duplicates  : 24,259 duplicate track_ids. The same song is often listed under
              several genres.

Target classes: classical, hip-hop, heavy-metal, country, edm

Cleaning and sampling steps:
  1. Dropped duplicate track_ids across the full dataset (keeping the first
     occurrence): 114,000 -> 89,741 rows. Without this, the same feature
     vector could appear in both train and test with different labels.
  2. Kept only the 5 target genres.
  3. Planned to sample 1,500 tracks per genre, but only 1,000 exist per genre
     (fewer after removing duplicates), so every remaining track was kept.

Final dataset: 4,346 tracks

    Genre         Tracks
    heavy-metal      997
    country          946
    classical        867
    hip-hop          842
    edm              694

The classes are slightly imbalanced (EDM has the fewest), so the split is
stratified and all metrics are macro-averaged (each genre counts equally).

Features used (10 audio features):
  danceability, energy, loudness, speechiness, acousticness,
  instrumentalness, liveness, valence, tempo, duration_ms

Excluded on purpose:
  * popularity and explicit: not properties of the sound
  * artist, album and track name: the model would learn to recognise artists
    instead of learning from the audio
  * key, mode and time_signature: categorical musical details that a user
    would not set in the app

What each genre looks like:
  * Classical: very different from the others. It is quiet (-19.9 dB versus
    about -6 dB for the rest), highly acoustic (0.92), mostly instrumental
    (0.60) and low in energy (0.20).
  * Heavy-metal: highest energy (0.87), almost no acoustic sound (0.015) and
    low danceability (0.43). This combination is unique to the genre.
  * Hip-hop: most danceable (0.73) and has by far the highest speechiness

  * EDM: loud, energetic and danceable, with the shortest tracks. It has no
    single standout feature, which is why it is the hardest to classify.
  * Country: average values on most features. It is moderately acoustic,
    has the lowest instrumentalness and has fairly high valence (happier
    sound).

Correlations between features:
  * energy and loudness are strongly positive (+0.85)
  * energy and acousticness are strongly negative (-0.86)
  * loudness and acousticness (-0.77) and loudness and instrumentalness
    (-0.74) are strongly negative
  * These features overlap a lot (the louder and more energetic a track is,
    the less acoustic it is). This mostly separates classical from the
    other four genres.
  * Tempo, liveness and duration are only weakly correlated with the other
    features.

4. METHODOLOGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  * Train/test split : 80/20, stratified by genre, random_state = 42
                       (3,476 training tracks, 870 test tracks)
  * Preprocessing    : each model is a Pipeline(StandardScaler -> classifier).
                       The scaler is fitted on the training data only, and
                       the app uses the same pipeline. Scaling matters for
                       LR, kNN and SVM and has no effect on the tree models.
  * Models           :
      - Logistic Regression (max_iter=2000)
      - Decision Tree (default settings)
      - Random Forest (200 trees)
      - k-Nearest Neighbours (k = 7)
      - SVM (RBF kernel, probability=True)
  * Metrics          : accuracy, and macro-averaged precision, recall and F1,
                       plus per-class reports and confusion matrices
  * Tuning           : GridSearchCV on Random Forest using 5-fold stratified
                       cross-validation on the training set only, scored by
                       macro F1, over 48 parameter combinations (240 fits)

Main points from the comparison:
  * Random Forest leads by about 6 F1 points over the next model (SVM) and
    about 9 points over the rest. Its lead is largest on the hard genres:
    EDM F1 is 0.775 compared with 0.59 to 0.65 for the other models.
  * Every model gets classical right (F1 above 0.92), so the models only
    really differ on hip-hop, EDM and country.
  * A single Decision Tree scores about as well as Logistic Regression and
    kNN, but it overfits. Averaging 200 to 400 trees (Random Forest) removes
    most of that variance and gives the 8-point improvement.
  * Logistic Regression has the weakest EDM score (0.587). Its straight-line
    decision boundaries cannot separate genres that overlap this much.
  * kNN does well on classical, which forms its own cluster, but poorly on
    genres that overlap in feature space.
  * The RBF kernel lets SVM draw curved boundaries, which puts it clearly
    ahead of LR, DT and kNN, but it still trails the Random Forest.

Hyperparameter tuning (Random Forest):
  Best parameters: n_estimators=400, max_depth=None, max_features='sqrt',
                   min_samples_split=2, min_samples_leaf=1
  Best cross-validation macro F1: 0.8518 (test-set macro F1: 0.8537)
  Apart from using 400 trees instead of 200, these are the default settings,
  so tuning gained only +0.0005 F1. The cross-validation score and the test
  score are almost the same, which suggests the model generalises well and
  is not overfitting the test split.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. KEY INSIGHTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Audio features alone are enough to predict genre fairly well. With no
   artist or title information, the best model is right 86% of the time
   across 5 genres (random guessing would be right 20% of the time).

2. Genre difficulty follows how different a genre sounds. Classical and
   heavy-metal are at the two ends of the energy and acoustic scale, so
   they are easy to separate. Hip-hop, EDM and country all fall in the same
   "loud, energetic, danceable" region, so they get confused with each
   other.

3. Hip-hop and EDM are the most confused pair (16 hip-hop tracks were
   predicted as EDM and 20 EDM tracks as hip-hop). Both are danceable,
   electronically produced and loud. Speechiness is the main feature that
   separates them.

4. Hip-hop is also often predicted as country (21 tracks). Modern hip-hop
   and pop-country have similar energy, valence and loudness, and the
   overlap between them is real.

5. Acousticness is the most important feature (0.236). It mainly separates
   classical (and, to a lesser degree, country) from the electronic and
   amplified genres. Danceability and speechiness are what separate
   hip-hop and EDM.

6. Tempo and liveness are weak predictors. All five genres average between
   108 and 127 BPM, so tempo does not tell them apart.

7. Ensemble tree models are the best fit for this data. The boundaries
   between genres are curved, the features interact (for example, high
   energy combined with low danceability means heavy-metal), and the
   features are on different scales. Random Forest handles all of this
   without needing manual feature engineering.

8. More tuning has little benefit. The default Random Forest was already
   near its best, so further gains would more likely come from better data
   (more tracks per genre, extra features like key or mode) than from
   tuning.

9. Removing duplicates was important. A quarter of the raw rows (24,259)
   were the same song under multiple genre labels. Leaving them in would
   have put identical tracks in both train and test sets and inflated the
   scores.

7. DEPLOYMENT (STREAMLIT APP)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  * All 6 pipelines are saved with joblib in models/, together with
    metadata.joblib (feature order, slider ranges, genre averages, test
    scores, best parameters and top tracks) and track_finder.joblib.
  * A check in the notebook reloads the saved tuned Random Forest and
    confirms it gives the same predictions as the in-memory model.
  * The app (app.py, run with "streamlit run app.py") provides:
      - sidebar sliders for the 10 audio features, limited to the ranges
        seen in training
      - predictions from every model side by side, with class probabilities
      - "songs that sound like this": real tracks whose audio is closest to
        the slider settings, found with a NearestNeighbors model on the
        scaled features
      - the top songs of each genre by popularity


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  * Small dataset: only about 870 usable tracks per genre, fewer than the
    1,500 planned. More data would likely help most with EDM and hip-hop.
  * Genre labels are subjective, and many songs belong to more than one
    genre. Keeping only each track's first label adds some noise to the
    labels.
  * Only 5 genres that sound fairly different were used. The same approach
    on all 125 genres would score much lower.
  * Possible improvements: gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM), adding key,
    mode and time_signature as features, class weights for EDM, and
    calibrating the SVM's probabilities (SVC(probability=True) is deprecated
    in newer scikit-learn versions).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Random Forest is the recommended model for this task, with 86.0% accuracy
and 0.854 macro F1. It is clearly better than SVM (0.791) and the simpler
models (about 0.76 to 0.77), and tuning confirmed that its default settings
were already close to the best. The analysis shows that genre can be
predicted from how a song sounds. Acousticness, danceability, energy and
speechiness carry most of the information, and the main remaining errors
are between hip-hop, EDM and country, which overlap a lot in their audio
features.
